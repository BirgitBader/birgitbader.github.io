_Why the technical writer's perspective belongs in the room before there's anything to document._

### The work is necessary and still comes last

My mind keeps wandering back to a time when I worked in a highly regulated environment, specifically in wind energy and mechanical engineering (hello, [European Machinery Directive](https://www.maschinenrichtlinie.de/fileadmin/dokumente/2006-42-EG%20Machinery%20Directive.pdf), my friend).
And since I’m daily concerned in my agile environment with how we can effectively ensure that technical writing supports the entire software development lifecycle, I’ve been wondering why, especially in an environment where documentation is mandatory, we accept that it ends up coming last and becomes a bottleneck.
This is a strange position technical writing often finds itself in, and I've noticed this in various jobs, industries, and companies.
The work is necessary, sometimes it is even mandatory.
Everyone knows it needs to happen, and yet, it still comes last.

### The thing that happens at the end

Think about a typical product development process in mechanical engineering:

- There are requirements and specifications.
- There is design and engineering.
- Prototypes are built and tested.
- There's risk assessment and conformity work: the technical file that has to exist before a machine can carry a CE mark.

And somewhere towards the end, someone writes the instructions for use, the one document the Machinery Directive names explicitly as part of that technical file, and requires in the language of every country the machine ships to.

Which sounds reasonable until you remember that this isn't a nice-to-have.
No instructions, no technical file, no CE mark, no legal sale.
So why is it treated as if it could wait until everything else is done?
Because that's when the pressure starts:

- The information arrives late.
- Terminology isn't always consistent.
- Things have changed since the first specifications were written.
- Different people use different words for the same thing.
- There are questions that nobody has had time to answer yet.
- The content has to be written, reviewed, translated and delivered.

And suddenly the documentation team is the bottleneck.
Not because writing itself necessarily takes so long, but a lot of thinking that could have happened earlier now has to happen at the same time as the writing.

### What happens before there is anything to write?

This is where I — again — come to the point: people are looking at the role from the wrong end.

The technical writing craft is usually associated with the thing that comes out of the process: the documentation, the guide, the manual, the page someone needs to read.
But the craft starts much earlier than that, see my earlier post [The strange case of the technical writer](/blog/the-strange-case-of-the-technical-writer/).

It starts when people are figuring out what they are actually building.
During requirements engineering, crafting the design, writing the specification. 
In the conversations where concepts are still being shaped and decisions are still open.
A technical writer doesn't need to be there to start writing.
Quite the opposite.

Being there early means listening, following the reasoning, asking the slightly annoying question that nobody else thought to ask.
They would notice that two people are using the same word differently and would realize that something makes perfect sense to the people building it but might be completely opaque to someone encountering it for the first time.

And perhaps most importantly, they would start to understand the product before there is a product to explain.

### Language has a funny way of becoming permanent

Terminology is a good example.
A new concept appears and someone gives it a name.
The name ends up in a specification, then in a presentation. 
Someone from Sales picks it up, Marketing uses it, customers hear it, other teams start using it because, well, that's what everyone is calling it now.
By the time technical writing gets involved, the terminology has already acquired a life of its own.

And then we discover that the same thing has three names.
Or that two different things have the same name. 
Or that the name made sense internally but is confusing to the people who are actually supposed to use the product.

Changing it at that point is hard.
It's not impossible, but it's expensive.

In a regulated environment it's more than expensive.
The instructions get translated into every language of every country the machine ships to, and an inconsistency in the source doesn't stay a single inconsistency, it gets multiplied once across every one of those languages.
And it's not just a helpful manual anymore, it's a legal document, so a mistranslated safety instruction isn't an annoyance, it's a liability question.

And terminology isn't the only thing that gets harder when the craft comes in late:

- Creating content that works for different audiences becomes harder when the audience hasn't been considered earlier.
- Information structures have to be retrofitted instead of designed with the eventual users in mind.

None of these problems suddenly appear when the technical writer opens the document.
They were already there.
We just didn't have someone looking at them from that perspective yet.

### This isn't about writing earlier

My point is not to move documentation further to the left on a process diagram and declare victory.
It is about bringing the technical writing perspective into the process earlier.
There is a difference.
A technical writer might sit in a risk assessment session and write nothing.
They might join a design review and leave without a single sentence of documentation.
But they might notice something important: a hazard being described one way now that will need to be described very precisely later, a term about to be used two different ways by two different engineers.

None of that is the final product, but it is part of the craft.

### Maybe the bottleneck is created much earlier

That's the part I keep coming back to, and that I would like to challenge especially in the places where documentation is mandatory:

When technical writing arrives at the end, it can look like the craft is slowing everything down.
We are the ones asking questions when everyone else wants to ship, we find inconsistencies when people thought they were finished, we are the ones saying, "Wait, what exactly does this mean?" when the deadline is suddenly very close.

I think the problem is that those questions are being asked for the first time at the end.
And that makes me wonder what would happen if technical writing was part of the process while people were still figuring things out?

That doesn't mean technical writers need a seat at every table, but that our perspective is useful before there is anything to write.
